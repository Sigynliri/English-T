Task 1. Listen and notice the grammar

Listen to the interview again. Find examples in which Lou asks about a candidate’s past experience and explains his own professional method.

1. Past accomplishment

Write down two questions Lou asks about a completed accomplishment.

Why does Lou use the Past Simple in these questions?

2. Articles with abstract nouns

Complete the phrases you hear:

Why is the used in these phrases, even though the nouns are abstract?

3. Describing a professional process

Complete the examples:

What does the Present Continuous communicate here?

4. If-sentences

“If I know they’re dominant and in your face and direct, that could be a problem.”

Underline or write the verb phrase after if, then identify the structure and explain what could means here.

Check your answers

Past accomplishment: “What was the pace like?”, “What was the intensity like?”, “Did you have enough resources?”, “What was the biggest decision you made?”, “How was it made?”, “Were you frustrated by that?”, “Did you like it?”, “What role did that person play in your success?”, “How did they delegate?”

Past Simple: Lou asks about a completed past experience and the actions, decisions and circumstances connected with it.

Articles: the pace of the organization; the level of sophistication; the depth of resources. The article marks a specific, identifiable aspect of the organization or situation. Abstract nouns can take articles.

Present Continuous: “I’m starting to get…”, “I’m mapping…”. Lou describes the actions and thought processes that form part of his professional method—not necessarily something he is learning right now.

Conditional: If + Present Simple, that could + infinitive. This is a real/open conditional describing a possible consequence; could means “might / it is possible that”. It is not a Second Conditional just because it contains could.

Task 2. Match the grammar to its function

Choose the best explanation for each example.

1. “What role did that person play?”

2. “I’m getting a sense of the pace…”

3. “I’m mapping it to the environment of my client.”

4. “If I know they’re dominant…, that could be a problem.”

Check your answers
  1. Asking about a completed past situation — Past Simple.
  2. Describing part of a professional process — Present Continuous.
  3. Comparing candidate information with the client’s environment as part of his method.
  4. A possible consequence of a real/open condition; could makes the result less definite.

Task 3. Past achievement → current relevance

Rewrite each idea so that it moves from a completed past action to what you do now. Use the Present Continuous in the second sentence.

  1. I created personalised materials for my students.
  2. I started working with young learners.
  3. I developed a system for organising homework.
  4. I began using AI tools to prepare materials.
  5. I started working with authentic video and audio.
Possible answers
  1. I’m currently using personalised materials to adapt my lessons to individual students’ needs.
  2. I’m working with young learners and adapting my teaching approach to their age and level.
  3. I’m using a structured system to organise homework and monitor students’ progress.
  4. I’m using AI tools to make material preparation more efficient.
  5. I’m incorporating authentic video and audio into my lessons.

Notice: I developed… = completed past action; I’ve developed… = experience/current result; I’m developing… = activity in progress. In Lou’s interview, I’m looking at / I’m asking / I’m mapping describes his professional method.

Task 4. Build an interview answer

Prepare a short answer using this sequence: past experience → experience accumulated up to now → current professional activity → relevance.

  1. Past Simple: What did you do or achieve?
  2. Present Perfect: What experience have you gained since then?
  3. Present Continuous: What are you doing / using in your work now?
  4. Relevance: Why does this matter for the role or learner?
Model answer
I previously worked with individual students and developed personalised materials for them. Since then, I’ve gained more experience in adapting lessons to different learning needs. At the moment, I’m using a more structured approach to lesson planning and incorporating more authentic materials. This is particularly relevant because it allows me to adapt my teaching to different learners and their goals.

Task 5. Sentence stress

Read aloud. The words in bold are examples of likely sentence stress. The exact stress can change with the speaker’s intended contrast or emphasis.

I’m GETTING a SENSE of the PACE of the ORGANIZATION.
I’m MAPPING it to the ENVIRONMENT of my CLIENT.
I’m getting a real SENSE of your WORK ENVIRONMENT.
If I know they’re DOMINANT and DIRECT, that could be a PROBLEM.

Now choose one sentence and record or repeat it three times, keeping the content words prominent and the grammatical words lighter.

Task 6. Chunking

Read the sentences in thought groups. Pause briefly at each slash; don’t pronounce every word as a separate unit.

I’m getting a sense / of the pace of the organization.
I’m mapping it / to the environment of my client.
I’m getting a real sense / of your work environment.
If I know they’re dominant / and in your face / and direct, / that could be a problem.

Transfer: finish the sentence about your own work, using the same pattern.

I’m getting a sense / of the students’ needs, / their level of confidence, / the pace they can work at, / and what kind of support they need.

Task 7. Weak forms

Read the phrases aloud. Keep the content words clear, while function words are usually shorter and less prominent in connected speech.

  • a sense of
  • the pace of
  • the environment of
  • a problem
  • what kind of
  • how much did you…?

Focus on: a, the, of, to, did, you, and. Don’t make every small grammatical word equally strong.

Task 8. Shadowing

Listen to a short excerpt and repeat it, trying to reproduce the speaker’s thought groups, sentence stress, weak forms, linking, rhythm and intonation.

  1. Listen once without speaking.
  2. Listen again and mark the thought groups with slashes.
  3. Repeat along with the speaker.
  4. Repeat without the audio.
  5. Transfer the pattern to a sentence about your own professional work.
I’m getting a sense of… / I’m mapping it to… / I’m looking at… / I’m asking…

Goal: use I’m + -ing to describe a professional action or method—not just imitate the speaker’s voice.

Task 9. Professional communication: retrieve the chunk

Try to remember the exact expression from the video. Use the clue to retrieve the missing word.

1. How do you professionally respond to unsolicited input?

Thank you for your ______.

Hint: a suggestion, opinion or piece of information someone offers to help or influence a decision.

2. How do you professionally point out that something falls within someone’s responsibility, while offering limited support?

Hints and model answers

1. input — “Thank you for your input.” This acknowledges the other person’s contribution and may politely close the topic; it does not literally mean “stay out of my business.”

2. “I understand that falls within your scope of responsibility, but I’m happy to support where it makes sense.”

Chunks: scope of responsibility = the area someone is responsible for; where it makes sense = where it is reasonable or appropriate.
